# Serpent

Serpent is a 128-bit block cipher with 128-, 192- and 256-bit keys, designed
by Ross Anderson, Eli Biham and Lars Knudsen and submitted to the AES
competition in June 1998. It is specified in the submission document
["Serpent: A Proposal for the Advanced Encryption
Standard"](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~rja14/serpent.html) (1998), which
supersedes the FSE 1998 paper "Serpent: A New Block Cipher Proposal" whose
S-boxes were different; it was one of the five finalists of the competition
that Rijndael won in October 2000, placed second in the finalists'
conference vote, and is unpatented and in the public domain by its
designers' declaration. It is used in disk encryption (TrueCrypt and its
successor VeraCrypt, and the Linux kernel's dm-crypt) and in libraries such
as Botan, Crypto++, libgcrypt and Nettle, and it is standardized neither by
NIST nor by the IETF. As of 2026 it is unbroken: the best published attacks
reach twelve of its thirty-two rounds.

## Analysis

### Structure

Serpent is a substitution-permutation network of thirty-two rounds on a
block of four 32-bit words. Round i xors the four words of the round key
K_i into the block, sends the block through the S-box S_(i mod 8), and
applies the linear transformation L; the last round replaces L with the xor
of a thirty-third round key K_32. There are eight 4-bit S-boxes S0 to S7,
each used in four rounds. L is a fixed sequence of rotations, shifts and
xors: X0 <<< 13, X2 <<< 3, X1 ^= X0 ^ X2, X3 ^= X2 ^ (X0 << 3), X1 <<< 1,
X3 <<< 7, X0 ^= X1 ^ X3, X2 ^= X3 ^ (X1 << 7), X0 <<< 5, X2 <<< 22, and it
mixes every output bit of the S-box layer into many S-boxes of the next
round. Decryption runs the inverse S-boxes and the inverse of L in the
reverse order with the same keys.

The proposal describes the cipher twice: in a "standard" form, where an
initial permutation IP moves bits so that each S-box acts on a 4-bit slice
of the block and a final permutation FP moves them back, and in a
"bitslice" form, the one its designers implemented, where the block is
kept as four words and bit j of the four words holds the j-th 4-bit S-box
input, so that all thirty-two S-boxes of a layer are one boolean function
evaluated on the words. IP and FP exist only to translate between the two
descriptions; in the bitslice form they are the identity, and this file
writes the bitslice form.

The key schedule takes a 256-bit key as eight little-endian words w_-8 to
w_-1; a shorter key is first padded with one 1 bit and then 0 bits to 256
bits, so a 128-bit key has w_-4 = 1 and a 192-bit key w_-2 = 1. It expands
them by the affine recurrence
w_i = (w_(i-8) ^ w_(i-5) ^ w_(i-3) ^ w_(i-1) ^ phi ^ i) <<< 11 with
phi = 0x9e3779b9, the fractional part of the golden ratio, into the 132
prekeys w_0 to w_131, and forms the round key K_i by applying
S_((3 - i) mod 8) bitsliced to (w_4i, w_4i+1, w_4i+2, w_4i+3): K_0 uses S3,
K_1 uses S2, down to K_3 with S0, then K_4 with S7 and around, so that K_32
uses S3.

The Orange file follows the proposal part by part; the proposal could not
be read from the machine this entry was written on, so its comments name
the parts rather than section numbers. A block is `Word[8]^16` read as
four little-endian words of the type `Block`, `Word[32]^4`, the bitslice
words X0 to X3; the round keys are `RoundKeys`, an array of thirty-three
blocks with K_i at index i; each S-box is one spec whose body is Osvik's
boolean form as a sequence of typed lets, and the linear transformation
is one let per line of the proposal's ten.

| Proposal part | Orange spec |
| --- | --- |
| The bitslice representation, a block as four little-endian words | `Block`; `as little Block` and `as little Word[8]^16` in `encrypt` and `decrypt` |
| The S-boxes S0 to S7, bitsliced | `sbox0` to `sbox7`, selected by round in `sbox` |
| Their inverses | `inverse_sbox0` to `inverse_sbox7`, selected in `inverse_sbox` |
| The linear transformation L and its inverse | `linear_transformation`, `inverse_linear_transformation` |

| Decryption | `inverse_round`, `decrypt` |

### Security status

Serpent was designed with a deliberately large margin. Its designers
argued in the proposal that sixteen rounds would already resist the
differential and linear attacks known in 1998 and specified thirty-two
rounds, twice that, so that the cipher would survive decades of
cryptanalysis; the S-boxes were generated by a published procedure from
the DES S-boxes under stated criteria (every differential characteristic
of an S-box has probability at most 1/4 and a one-bit input difference
never gives a one-bit output difference; every linear characteristic has
bias at most 1/4), and the linear transformation was chosen so that a
difference spreads to many S-boxes within a few rounds, with the bounds
on characteristics given in the proposal. The AES evaluation confirmed
the margin: NIST's Round 2 report (Nechvatal et al., October 2000) rated
Serpent, Twofish and MARS as having a high security margin and Rijndael
and RC6 an adequate one.

The published attacks on reduced-round Serpent form a steady record that
has never come near the full cipher. Kelsey, Kohno and Schneier's
amplified boomerang attack (FSE 2000) reached eight rounds; Biham,
Dunkelman and Keller's linear cryptanalysis (FSE 2001) reached ten and
eleven rounds and their rectangle attack (Eurocrypt 2001) ten; their
differential-linear attack (FSE 2003) reached eleven rounds for all key
sizes, and Dunkelman, Indesteege and Keller extended it to twelve rounds
of Serpent-256 (Indocrypt 2008) at about 2^123 chosen plaintexts and 2^249
time. Multidimensional linear cryptanalysis (Nguyen, Wu and Wang, ACISP
2011, refining earlier multiple-linear attacks by Collard, Standaert and
Quisquater, 2007 and 2008) also reaches twelve rounds of Serpent-256, and
McLaughlin and Clark's filtered nonlinear cryptanalysis (2013) eleven and
twelve rounds with marginal gains. The attack complexities named here are
recalled from the literature and not checked from this machine; the round
counts are the ones the field agrees on. Twelve of thirty-two rounds, at
complexities near the key size, leave a margin of twenty rounds, the
largest among the AES finalists; no related-key, algebraic or structural
attack on the full cipher has been published.

Serpent lost the competition on performance rather than security. Its
thirty-two rounds make it roughly half the speed of Rijndael in software
on the general-purpose processors of 2000, and NIST's report recorded it
as the slowest of the finalists on most platforms, while ranking it well
in hardware, where the bitsliced S-boxes are small and fast, and noting
its simplicity of analysis; the finalists' conference straw poll placed it
second to Rijndael. Its bitsliced construction, inspired by Biham's 1997
bitslice implementation of DES, was ahead of its time in one respect: a
cipher whose S-boxes are boolean gate sequences on words has no
data-dependent table lookup, so a straightforward implementation runs in
constant time and is immune to the cache-timing attacks on table-driven
AES demonstrated by Bernstein (2005) and Osvik, Shamir and Tromer (2006),
a property that table-based AES implementations had to recover through
hardware instructions or bitslicing of their own.

Its status in 2026: unbroken; in use in disk encryption (the TrueCrypt
lineage and VeraCrypt offer it alone and in cascades with AES and Twofish,
and dm-crypt supports it) and in general-purpose libraries; standardized
neither by NIST nor by the IETF, which have no Serpent cipher suites or
modes; and free of patents. Two practical points are not weaknesses of the
cipher but matter to its use. The block is 128 bits, so a single block
evaluated as here is not a mode of operation, and security in use depends
on the mode and on IV discipline. And Serpent's byte order was a source of
confusion: the proposal states words little-endian, the AES submission's
original known-answer files used the opposite convention, and the
designers later declared the little-endian one canonical, which is the one
NESSIE, Botan and this entry follow (the history of the submission files
is recalled and not checked from this machine).

### What the Orange rendering shows

Nothing in Serpent is data-dependent. In the bitsliced form the S-boxes
are boolean formulas on the four words, the linear transformation is
rotations, shifts and xors by literal amounts, and the key schedule is the
same operations on the prekeys, with its one rotation, by 11, as fixed as
the proposal writes it. The only conditionals in the file, the S-box
selections `sbox` and `inverse_sbox`, choose an S-box by the round number,
an `Int` fixed by the loop, never by the data. Every index is a literal or
a loop index: the prekey array holds w_-8 to w_131 with w_i at index
i + 8, so the recurrence reads `w[i]`, `w[i + 3]`, `w[i + 5]` and
`w[i + 7]`; round key K_i is the slice `w[4 * i + 8..4 * i + 12]` passed
through its S-box and stored as row i of `RoundKeys`; encryption reads
`k[i]` and decryption `k[30 - j]`. The checker proves each in range before
evaluation. This is the cipher its designers wrote, and it needs no
lookup: the file has no table at all.

Byte order is written once per place the proposal fixes it: the plaintext
or ciphertext becomes four words with `as little Block` and the result
goes back to bytes with `as little Word[8]^16`, and the padded key becomes
w_-8 to w_-1 with `as little Word[32]^8`. Key lengths are sizes:
`pad_key[len]` has one instance per key length from 1 through 31 bytes,
appending the fill `[0; (32 - len)]` whose first byte is `0x01`, the
proposal's single 1 bit in little-endian order; a test passes its 16- or
24-byte key and the checker picks the instance from its length, while a
256-bit key goes to `encrypt` and `decrypt` as it is. The tests write keys,
plaintexts and ciphertexts as `hex"..."` in the order Botan's file prints
them.

The correspondence between the two descriptions is stated rather than
computed: IP and FP are the identity here, and the file says so and does
not carry the permutation tables. The S-box boolean forms are Osvik's
rather than a transcription from the proposal's tables; each was verified
against its table on all sixteen inputs, so a reader who trusts the tables
can trust the formulas, but the formulas are not the proposal's text. The
linear transformation is the proposal's own sequence of ten updates, each
a `let` naming the word after its update, and its inverse names each
word by the forward `let` it recovers.

Measured costs under `orangec test --stats` and `orangec eval --stats`:
a call to one bitsliced S-box costs 67 to 83 steps for all thirty-two
nibbles of a block, and selecting it through `sbox` adds 10 steps for S0
up to 42 for S7; L costs 55 steps and its inverse 59; a round 170 to 209.
The prekeys cost 5,295 steps and the whole key schedule 9,225. Encryption
of one block costs 14,832 steps with its key schedule, about 5,600 without
it, and decryption 15,218, about 6,000 without it. The twelve tests
together use 179,232 steps, 14,834 to 15,228 each.

Not expressed: constant-time behaviour (the evaluator specifies values,
not timing), any mode of operation, and the standard (non-bitsliced)
description with IP and FP.

### Provenance and claims

The proposal could not be read from the machine this entry was written on,
so every constant was taken from fetched reference material and
cross-checked by script, never transcribed by eye or from memory:

- The S-box boolean forms came from Botan's `serpent_sbox.h` (Osvik's
  expressions, `SBoxE0` to `SBoxE7` and `SBoxD0` to `SBoxD7`). A script
  interpreted each statement sequence on bitsliced 4-bit inputs to recover
  its 16-entry table, checked that each forward table is a permutation and
  that each `SBoxD` table is the inverse of its `SBoxE`, and confirmed the
  tables are those of the proposal (S0 = 3 8 15 1 10 6 5 11 14 13 4 2 7 0
  9 12, and so on). The same script generated the sixteen Orange specs by
  renaming each assignment into a `let`; a scratch Orange file applied
  every generated spec to the bitsliced identity input and matched the
  result against the table under `orangec eval` before the specs entered
  the file.
- The linear transformation, its inverse, phi = 0x9e3779b9, the key
  padding with a 1 bit, the prekey recurrence and the S-box order of the
  round keys came from Botan's `serpent_fn.h` and `serpent.cpp` and agree
  with the proposal as recalled.
- A table-based Python reference in the proposal's vocabulary (the S-box
  tables applied nibble by nibble, L, the prekeys, K_i), built from those
  constants, reproduces all 1,047 records of `serpent.vec` on encryption
  and decryption, including the four multi-block records. The Orange specs
  were written from the proposal's definitions and that reference, and
  the twelve vectors matched on the first evaluation.

The Python reference, the S-box interpreter and generator, the vector
classifier and literal generator, and the step-measurement drivers were
kept with the work record and are not part of the repository.

The entry was then rewritten in the current language. Every expected value
is carried over byte for byte from the first form, where each was a
`<name>_expected` spec holding an array of byte literals: the twelve tests
state the same sixteen bytes as `hex"..."`, and so do the keys and
plaintexts, compared byte for byte with the first form's arrays (the
decryption tests' zero and single-bit blocks, formerly specs, are the same
bytes). No vector was added or dropped. The sixteen S-box specs are the
first form's text unchanged but for the name of their type; the linear
transformation and its inverse were split into one `let` per line of the
proposal, the key padding became one sized spec for every key length, the
round keys an array of thirty-three blocks, and the hand-written byte
loads `as little`, all checked against the same twelve vectors.

This entry is a reference evaluation of the Serpent proposal under
`orangec test`. It makes no constant-time, side-channel, performance or
certification claim, and it is not a corpus entry in the sense of The
Orange Book chapter 12.

## Gaps

- A spec is not a value and the language has no arrays of functions, so
  the S-box of a round is chosen by an eight-arm conditional on `r % 8`
  in `sbox` and `inverse_sbox` rather than by indexing a list of the eight
  S-boxes.
- This rendering takes keys of whole bytes: `pad_key` takes 1 through 31
  bytes. The padding of a key whose bit length is not a multiple of 8 is
  not written, nor that of an empty key, since an array has at least one
  element.
